# LOGIC NEXUS — MASTER SCRIPT (voice pass)
## **Could L Catch Spider-Man?**

**Continuity:** MCU Peter Parker after *No Way Home*
**Narration begins at 0:00. No intro. No logo. No music-only lead-in.**

*Voice pass only. The story, beats and order are the same as the original. What changed: present tense throughout, no report-speak, far fewer "Not… / That… / Nothing." lines, and far less hedging.*

---

### 0:00 — HOOK

The whole world forgot Peter Parker.

L doesn't need them to remember.

Because every night Spider-Man puts on the mask, he makes the same mistake.

He acts like Peter Parker.

He picks which crimes to stop. He picks which way to go home. He keeps coming back to the same parts of New York. He uses gear somebody has to build, fix and replace. And he disappears for hours at a time, because under that mask there's still a guy with a life.

L has cracked cases with a lot less.

But Peter's got something L has never had to deal with.

Spider-Sense.

It tells him something's wrong before he even knows what.

So L can't just tail him.

He can't sneak up on him.

And the second Peter figures out someone's studying Spider-Man, every pattern L finds turns into a pattern Peter can fake.

So this investigation doesn't stay an investigation for long.

It turns into a detective rebuilding a man the whole world forgot…

against a superhero who realizes someone's rebuilding him.

And here's the strange part.

L can figure out exactly who Spider-Man is…

and still have no way to prove it.

So let's hand L the case.

No Avengers files.

No magic explanation for Doctor Strange's spell.

Nobody who remembers that Peter Parker was Spider-Man.

Just the evidence New York already has.

Could L catch Spider-Man?

Let's find out.

# THE FIRST 48 HOURS

L's first move decides the whole case.

And his first move is to skip the obvious one.

He doesn't search for Spider-Man's identity. Not yet.

Search eight million people for a guy in a mask and you get eight million suspects.

So L rebuilds Spider-Man's life instead.

And the first thing he wants isn't web fluid.

It's time.

He pulls every confirmed Spider-Man sighting from the last few months. Police reports. 911 calls. Clear phone videos. News choppers. Traffic cams. Anything with a time and a place he can actually check.

Then he throws most of it out.

Fake sightings. Gone.

Random posts with no proof. Gone.

Guys in Halloween costumes. Gone.

L doesn't want more data. He wants data he can trust.

And when he lines up what's left, something jumps out.

Spider-Man isn't always around.

There are gaps.

Big ones.

Sounds like nothing, until you remember what this guy can do.

He crosses half of New York without ever hitting traffic. He hears trouble from places cops can't even get to. He can go for hours.

So why does he keep vanishing?

L breaks the week into hours.

Weekdays against weekends.

Everyday street stuff against real disasters.

And a pattern starts to show.

When something huge happens, Spider-Man shows up whenever.

But regular patrols keep a schedule.

Big disasters tell L where Spider-Man **can** go.

Regular patrols tell him where Spider-Man **chooses** to go.

So those gaps aren't about what he can do.

They're about his life.

Spider-Man sleeps.

Spider-Man eats.

Maybe he's got a job.

Maybe he's in school.

Maybe he's got stuff going on that makes Spider-Man disappear for a while.

L writes his first note:

**The guy under the mask controls the schedule.**

And right away, he tries to prove himself wrong.

Do the gaps line up with police shift changes? Weather? Traffic? Crime rates?

A few do.

Most don't.

The human schedule is still there.

So now L has something.

No name. No address.

A rhythm.

And you can hide your face a lot easier than you can hide the rhythm of your life.

But then L spots a problem.

Spider-Man's rhythm lately looks different from his old one.

Something changed.

The question is what.

---

# THE SPIDER-MAN BEFORE AND AFTER

L splits Spider-Man's history into chapters.

He doesn't need to understand aliens or magic or the multiverse. He just needs to compare how the guy acts.

Old Spider-Man footage shows a hero in a much bigger world.

Huge public fights.

Teaming up with other powered people.

Way fancier gear.

Way more out in the open.

Then something big hits New York, and all of that changes.

Today's Spider-Man is quieter.

More alone.

More local.

His gear looks like something he's keeping alive himself, with none of the big-money tech he used to have.

And he doesn't act the same in public anymore.

L isn't reading it emotionally. Not yet.

For him it's a supply problem.

Something cut Spider-Man off from whoever used to back him.

Maybe by choice. Maybe not.

For L, that's great news.

Because a guy with powerful friends is hard to corner.

A guy on his own has to handle regular-person problems himself.

Fixing his gear.

Replacing a torn suit.

Finding somewhere to sleep.

Finding money.

Healing up.

Eating enough food to keep a body like that running.

L isn't hunting Spider-Man's powers.

He's hunting Spider-Man's bills.

And that brings him to the suit.

The recent footage shows a suit one really smart kid could keep running on his own.

So L asks a simple question.

If Spider-Man rips his suit tonight…

what happens tomorrow?

There's no superhero costume store.

Somebody fixes it.

If his gear breaks, somebody repairs it.

If a special part fails, somebody replaces it.

And if Spider-Man's alone now, that somebody is probably Spider-Man.

Now L has a real profile.

Young. You can hear it in how he talks and moves.

Seriously good with tech.

Probably an engineer at heart.

Maybe a chemist too.

No big organization backing him.

A messy schedule.

Less money than he used to have.

And he almost definitely lives close to where he patrols the most.

That's still thousands of people.

Maybe tens of thousands.

But L isn't looking for Peter yet.

He's looking for the next filter.

So he asks a question nobody else chasing Spider-Man thinks to ask.

**When does Spider-Man choose NOT to help?**

# THE CRIMES SPIDER-MAN MISSES

Everybody else studies Spider-Man's rescues.

L studies the ones he skipped.

He grabs every reported incident Spider-Man could've reached and asks which ones he never showed up to.

At first it's a mess.

The guy can't be everywhere.

Half the time he probably never even heard about it.

But with enough cases, L starts comparing *could have* with *did*.

Two robberies, same distance away.

Spider-Man stops one and skips the other.

Why?

A car crash happens right near where he's swinging, but the cops beat him there.

Another crash is way farther away, and he's on it almost instantly.

Why?

L won't assume Spider-Man knows everything.

So he comes up with a few competing theories.

Maybe Spider-Man listens to police radio.

Maybe he watches public emergency alerts.

Maybe he just patrols and reacts to whatever he sees.

Maybe people reach out to him somehow.

Or maybe his powers include some kind of sense nobody understands.

Each theory predicts different behavior.

So L tests them.

No fake hostage situation.

Nobody's life on the line.

Just a few well-placed tips.

With the authorities' help, he slips a handful of harmless fake alerts into different channels over several nights.

One only goes out over a monitored police channel.

One gets out through public reports.

One's only visible from a spot Spider-Man just swung past.

And one looks serious on paper, but there's nothing actually dangerous there.

L watches.

Spider-Man ignores the first one.

Shows up weirdly fast to the second.

Never goes near the third.

Then, during another test, something happens that breaks every theory.

Spider-Man turns toward the scene before the alert has even had time to spread through the channel L thought he was using.

L checks the timestamps.

Then checks them again.

Spider-Man reacted too early.

And that bugs him.

So he runs the test again under different conditions.

Same weird result.

It's still no proof of precognition.

To L, that idea sounds ridiculous.

But it's enough to prove Spider-Man knows things L can't see.

And that flips the case.

If Spider-Man has some kind of built-in early warning, normal surveillance stops working.

A tail might get spotted.

An ambush might fail.

A hidden watcher might tip him off just by being there.

L writes his second note:

**Spider-Man doesn't see the world the way I do.**

So the investigation has to go sideways.

And that's where L gets dangerous.

Peter's biggest advantage is that he can sense danger.

So L's plan is simple.

Stop being dangerous.

# L BUILDS A CITY-SIZED EXPERIMENT

Instead of following Spider-Man, L studies what he leaves behind.

No webs. No footprints.

Decisions.

He splits New York into zones and compares how Spider-Man reacts in each one.

Where does he show up super fast?

Where does he show up late?

Where does he come in from above?

Where does he seem to come up from the street?

Where does he vanish and never show up on another camera?

L's not looking for one route home.

Peter would just switch it up.

He's looking for friction.

Spider-Man's powers make the city easier to cross, but some parts of the city are a pain.

Some areas have great height.

Others force awkward jumps.

Some spots are private.

Others are covered in cameras.

Some routes are fast but everybody sees you.

Others are slow but safe.

L combines all of that with time.

When a patrol starts matters.

When it ends matters even more.

In the middle of the night, Spider-Man could be anywhere.

But the start and end of a patrol are where his real life leaks through.

L notices the last sightings don't point to one building.

Peter's too careful for that.

But they're not random either.

They form a few wide corridors.

Now L has a hunting zone.

And then he does something you wouldn't expect.

He stops narrowing it down.

Narrow too fast and you start bending the evidence to fit your theory.

So he builds three competing guesses about where Spider-Man lives and waits to see which one holds up.

Days go by.

One weakens.

One's still possible.

The third keeps looking better and better.

Then Spider-Man does something strange.

His pattern changes.

Overnight.

His go-to corridors stop predicting anything.

His last sightings of the night start scattering.

His patrol times get all over the place.

Routes that made perfect sense get dumped for ones that make none.

L looks at the change and sees two options.

Either Spider-Man's life just flipped upside down…

or Spider-Man knows somebody's studying him.

And if it's the second one…

the game's already started.

# PETER REALIZES HE'S BEING MEASURED

Peter has no idea L exists.

But Peter Parker isn't dumb.

And Spider-Sense doesn't need to hand him a memo.

It just has to make a few things feel off.

A police response that's a little too organized.

A harmless incident with way too many cameras pointed at all the right spots.

An emergency alert that doesn't match what Peter finds when he gets there.

Once, you shrug it off.

Twice, you raise an eyebrow.

Three times, you start keeping score.

Peter starts replaying the last few nights in his head.

None of it's designed to hurt him.

And somehow that's the creepy part.

Villains want something.

Money.

Revenge.

Power.

This is different.

Someone's asking questions.

How fast does Spider-Man show up?

Where's he coming from?

What does he react to?

Where does he go after?

And Peter lands on the scary answer.

Nobody's attacking him.

Somebody's **measuring** him.

And if somebody's measuring Spider-Man, every normal patrol is free intel.

So Peter does exactly what L was afraid of.

He changes.

He takes longer routes on purpose.

He leaves Manhattan one way, loops back, then heads out another.

He changes when he patrols.

Sometimes he changes clothes far from home and just takes the train like everybody else.

He stops jumping at every suspicious call.

And most importantly, Peter starts creating fake patterns on purpose.

Four nights in a row, Spider-Man ends his patrol near the same part of the city.

It's not where Peter actually lives.

It's a decoy.

He makes sure the route looks natural.

No weird turns.

No looking over his shoulder.

He just lets the pattern exist.

Then he waits.

If someone's really studying him, Peter figures something around that area will change.

More cameras, maybe.

Cars he doesn't recognize.

People in regular clothes hanging around a little too long.

Something.

Two nights later, Peter spots it.

A camera's been turned.

Just a little.

Just enough to cover an exit that used to be a blind spot.

Peter almost smiles.

Now he knows.

Somebody took the bait.

But here's what Peter doesn't know…

L *wanted* him to notice.

Because L had worked out the same thing from the other side.

If Spider-Man's smart enough to notice he's being profiled, sooner or later he'll test the profiler.

So L lets one camera move get spotted on purpose.

Now they both know the other one's thinking.

And from here on, the whole case flips.

L isn't just studying Spider-Man anymore.

Spider-Man's studying L.

# THE FALSE NEIGHBORHOOD

Peter makes the first big move.

If the investigator wants geography, Peter's gonna give him geography.

Over the next week, Spider-Man quietly leans his movements toward a different neighborhood.

He doesn't go there every night.

Too obvious.

He builds the odds, little by little.

His first sightings of the night pop up nearby a bit more often.

His late-night disappearances sometimes point that way.

He uses rooftops that make the area look like a natural stop.

He even lets far-off cameras catch him heading that way after patrol.

Peter isn't trying to make it certain.

He's trying to make it look like exactly what an analyst loves:

a pattern that looks convincing on paper.

And it works.

L's model starts favoring the fake zone.

Now L starts searching for people who match his profile.

Young.

Good with tech.

Flexible schedule.

Not much money.

Into science or engineering.

One person starts to stand out.

The fit isn't perfect.

But real cases never are.

L quietly compares the guy's movements to Spider-Man's.

A few things line up.

Then another.

Then another.

For the first time, L has a real person on the board.

Peter just made him up a suspect.

And this is where a sloppier detective loses the case.

Because once you've got someone who looks right, every new fact starts looking like proof.

L can fall for it too.

But he knows the trap is there.

So instead of trying to prove the guy *is* Spider-Man…

he tries to prove he **isn't**.

And he pulls it off.

There's a confirmed Spider-Man sighting at a time when this guy was provably somewhere else.

Clear timestamp.

Solid witness.

Two people. Two places.

Suspect cleared.

Peter wins that round.

But L doesn't toss the failed theory.

He studies it.

Why did the wrong guy look so right?

The answer matters more than the suspect.

Because the fake neighborhood didn't come out of random noise.

It showed up right after Spider-Man's behavior changed.

So someone probably built it on purpose.

L compares the fake pattern to Spider-Man's older movements.

It's almost too clean.

It's exactly what someone tracking him on a map would want to see.

The target didn't just hide.

He handed L a story.

L leans in closer to the screen.

Spider-Man knows enough about the investigation to steer where it goes.

And that tells L something new.

Peter thinks he just sent the detective the wrong way.

Actually…

he just showed L how his mind works.

# THE INVESTIGATION TURNS INSIDE OUT

L drops the question of where Spider-Man lives.

For now.

The map's been poisoned.

Any route after Peter noticed the surveillance could be fake.

So L splits his data in two.

Everything **before** Spider-Man got suspicious goes in one pile.

Everything after goes in another.

The second pile doesn't show Spider-Man's normal life anymore.

It shows his game plan.

And that changes the whole approach.

Before he noticed, Spider-Man cared about two things: stopping crime and getting home.

After he noticed, he cared about fooling whoever was watching.

L compares the two piles.

What did Spider-Man change right away?

His routes.

His timing.

How he responds to calls.

What *didn't* he change?

His values.

That's L's next way in.

Peter can pick where he swings.

He can pick when he patrols.

He can even pick which camera sees him.

But changing his gut instinct to help people? Way harder.

L goes back through real moments where Spider-Man had to make a choice.

No setups this time.

Real emergencies already on record.

A suspect running one way.

A hurt civilian the other way.

Spider-Man lets the suspect go.

Something dangerous he could lock down, but somebody nearby needs help.

He picks the person.

Again.

Again.

Again.

Spider-Man's priorities never move.

People's lives first.

Catching the bad guy second.

His own safety way, way down the list.

Now L knows any test built around greed, ego or self-preservation is weak.

Spider-Man might just ignore it.

But responsibility?

That's different.

L could use that.

Before he can, though, a new problem shows up.

Peter's started looking for *him*.

# PETER HUNTS THE INVESTIGATOR

Peter knows an operation like this costs money and people.

Cameras don't turn themselves.

Police don't quietly change how they work without somebody giving orders.

Fake alerts don't write themselves.

Somewhere, someone's running this.

So Peter stops asking, "How are they following me?"

He starts asking, "Who's getting the data?"

He watches the cops after incidents instead of the criminals.

Which evidence gets picked up weirdly fast?

Which officers are getting orders that don't match normal procedure?

Which cameras still matter after Spider-Man's gone?

Peter doesn't have to break into anything.

He just follows the trail.

One surveillance team reports to another.

Everything moves up the chain.

Names vanish behind layers of clearance.

Peter keeps going.

But the closer he gets, the weirder it gets.

There's no normal investigator in the middle of it.

No detective publicly on the Spider-Man case.

No obvious task-force boss.

No face that keeps showing up.

Whoever's running this has basically erased themselves.

So Peter knows this isn't some regular cop.

So he runs another test.

On one patrol, he lets a camera catch what looks like one of his web-shooters glitching out.

He switches wrists.

Shakes the thing.

Then swings off.

It's fine. It was never broken.

For the next few days, Peter watches for changes.

If the investigator buys it, they'll probably build the next test around it.

And sure enough…

a setup appears in a spot where losing one web-shooter would actually matter.

Peter has his answer.

The investigator saw the footage.

Bought the glitch.

And adjusted.

Now Peter knows something about his opponent.

They don't just collect evidence.

They design experiments with it.

And L learns something about Peter at the exact same time.

Because when Spider-Man shows up to that spot…

the "broken" web-shooter works perfectly.

L stares at the footage.

The glitch was fake.

Peter fed him bad intel just to see if the investigation would react.

Both of them just ran experiments on each other…

at the same time.

And both experiments worked.

Now L sees how big the problem really is.

If he keeps testing Spider-Man directly, Peter will test the tests.

He needs a way in that Peter has no idea about.

So L goes backward.

Away from Spider-Man's life right now.

Into his past.

# THE PERSON WHO SHOULD BE THERE

This is where Doctor Strange's spell gets interesting.

L has no clue magic wiped Peter Parker from everyone's memory.

He doesn't even know what he's looking for.

He just notices Spider-Man's history has holes in it.

Spider-Man didn't show up yesterday.

He's got years of public history.

Stuff from when he was still in school.

Huge battles.

Public incidents.

Run-ins with all kinds of people and institutions.

So L asks a pretty simple question.

If Spider-Man was this young back then…

where was his normal life while all of this was going on?

He rebuilds everything in order.

He's not trying to spot Spider-Man directly.

He's trying to spot what's missing.

Picture a group photo where everyone is turned a little toward an empty spot.

One photo like that? Means nothing.

Hundreds of them, all turned toward the same empty spot?

Now you've got a story.

L finds events where the official explanation doesn't add up.

Times Spider-Man shows up somewhere as if he knew in advance, when he had no reason to.

The same groups of people keep ending up near Spider-Man events.

Records explain what happened, but never quite how these people ended up involved.

The name "Peter Parker" is nowhere.

And that's the whole point.

L starts to suspect this isn't just some anonymous guy.

Maybe the record itself is missing a piece.

He tries the normal explanations first.

A classified identity.

Deleted records.

Witness protection.

Government meddling.

A really good hack.

None of them explain everything.

The records aren't just missing.

People act like the connection never existed at all.

L doesn't jump to magic.

He doesn't have to.

He just calls it an unknown cause.

**Something happened that messed up the record of Spider-Man's real-life relationships.**

Now he asks when.

He compares when Spider-Man's behavior changed with when the holes in the history get worst.

They line up.

Now L has a dividing line.

There's a Spider-Man before this event…

and a Spider-Man after.

Before: connected.

After: alone.

Before: backed up.

After: on his own.

Before: a real life, tangled up with other people's lives.

After: almost nothing.

Somebody didn't just hide Spider-Man's identity.

Something cut it away.

And if L can find a real life that seems to *start* right at that break…

he finds the guy under the mask.

# PETER PARKER

L goes looking for people who fit the gap.

Plenty of people have incomplete records, so that alone is useless.

He stacks up his filters.

Right age range.

Right city.

Seriously gifted at science.

Not much money.

A real-life footprint that gets strangely thin right when Spider-Man goes solo.

And close to the clean map data from before Peter started faking things.

The list shrinks.

Then one name holds on longer than the rest.

Peter Parker.

On paper, Peter isn't Spider-Man.

He's barely anybody.

That's exactly what makes him interesting.

A young guy with big academic potential.

Living on almost nothing.

Living in exactly the situation L's profile predicted.

And his life right now feels cut off from the past that should've made him who he is.

L isn't ready to call Peter Spider-Man.

Not yet.

He sets up two possibilities.

Option one:

Peter Parker *is* Spider-Man.

Option two:

Peter Parker is *connected* to Spider-Man.

Maybe he builds the gear.

Maybe he knows who Spider-Man is.

Maybe Spider-Man looks out for him.

Maybe Peter's just another person that same mystery event hit.

L needs to rule one out.

So he does something Peter doesn't see coming.

He goes quiet.

No new traps.

No camera changes.

No strange police behavior.

The whole investigation seems to go silent.

For a few days, Peter starts wondering if he finally lost whoever was on his tail.

Which is exactly what L wants.

Because the safest time to watch a careful target…

is when he thinks nobody's watching anymore.

L compares Peter's everyday schedule with Spider-Man's activity using far-off, indirect sources.

Nobody tailing Peter around the block.

Nobody parked outside his place.

Just timestamps from different places.

Peter shows up somewhere.

Later, Spider-Man shows up somewhere else.

Spider-Man disappears.

Peter's normal day picks back up.

Once, that's a coincidence.

Twice, that's interesting.

Over and over, that's hard to ignore.

But something bugs L.

There's never one clean switch.

No clear footage.

No moment where Peter walks into an alley and Spider-Man swings out.

Peter's too careful.

And Spider-Sense makes getting close a real risk.

L's confidence climbs.

His proof stays flat.

Then Peter realizes the silence is fake.

Because going quiet is the last thing the person he's been studying would do.

Someone this patient doesn't just quit.

Peter gets it.

The investigation didn't stop.

It went dark.

And if he can't see what L is measuring…

he can't mess up the measurements.

For the first time, Peter's actually behind.

# PETER'S BEST MOVE

Peter needs to kill the main theory.

Confusing it isn't enough.

Kill it.

If the investigator thinks Peter Parker is Spider-Man, there's one obvious way to shut that down.

Show them both in two places at the same time.

Peter plans it carefully.

He picks a time when a bunch of unrelated sources can confirm where he is.

One camera isn't enough.

It'd look staged.

He needs witnesses.

Receipts.

A normal reason to be somewhere for a good while.

Meanwhile, Spider-Man has to show up somewhere else.

How Peter pulls that off matters less than the result.

A stand-in.

Some kind of remote setup.

Old footage slipped into the mix.

Some trusted way to make a believable Spider-Man sighting happen.

The investigator sees exactly what Peter wants.

Peter Parker, clearly in one place.

Spider-Man, clearly somewhere else.

For a few hours, L's best theory looks like it's falling apart.

And Peter doesn't celebrate.

He waits.

Because if the investigation shifts after this, he'll know they really did suspect him.

Nothing changes.

A day goes by.

Then another.

Peter starts to think he might've actually won.

But L has a problem with the evidence.

It's too convenient.

Up until now, Peter Parker's everyday records have been painfully boring.

Then suddenly, right after the investigation goes quiet, a big, clean contradiction shows up.

Peter and Spider-Man separated just cleanly enough to knock down the exact theory L was testing.

By itself, it proves nothing.

But it raises a question:

Why now?

L looks closely at that Spider-Man sighting.

He's not asking if it *looked* real.

He's asking if Spider-Man *acted* real.

This is where all those weeks of studying how Spider-Man acts pay off.

You can copy moves.

You can copy a costume.

You can even fake an appearance.

Faking decisions is a lot harder.

The Spider-Man in this sighting never has to make a real choice.

No second threat.

No thinking on his feet.

No random civilian in trouble.

Nothing that would test the priorities L has spent weeks tracking.

The sighting proves someone *looked* like Spider-Man.

It doesn't prove Spider-Man was there.

So L keeps Peter on the board.

If anything, he's more sure than before.

Because Peter's given him something more revealing than an alibi.

He's given him an alibi that looks made for this exact investigation.

And Peter realizes his mistake.

The investigator was never asking:

"Can Peter Parker be in one place while Spider-Man's in another?"

He was asking:

"What will Peter Parker do if he thinks I suspect him?"

And Peter answered.

# THE CASE L CANNOT PROVE

By now, L privately believes Peter is Spider-Man.

Imagine L laying out his case.

Peter's young.

Still no proof.

but he can't prove it.

He never changes somewhere he could be seen.

He never keeps the suit anywhere obvious.

He never goes straight from Peter to Spider-Man.

He never starts a patrol somewhere predictable.

And he doesn't jump at every suspicious tip just because Spider-Man normally would.

Peter locks in.

And for the first time, L starts running out of new evidence.

Spider-Man still shows up.

Peter still exists.

Spider-Sense might pick it up.

A trap for Peter Parker, not Spider-Man.

Spider-Sense can't just point him to the watcher.

And the test has to make Peter choose between protecting his secret…

and the one rule L knows he almost never breaks.

Responsibility.

This is where it stops being a forensics problem.

It becomes a mind game.

# L'S FINAL EXPERIMENT

L won't stage a real disaster.

Too risky, and it would wreck the whole point of a controlled test.

He controls what Peter hears instead.

He sets up a situation where Peter gets a believable reason to think a preventable accident is about to happen nearby.

Peter. Not Spider-Man.

The word reaches him through something totally normal, just part of his everyday life.

Nothing says it's a test.

Nothing says anyone's watching.
