# What If YOU Faked Your Own Death? Could L Catch You?
## What If YOU Were The Suspect? — Episode 001

**COLD OPEN**

You've thought about it. Everyone has, at some point — just disappearing, starting over, letting the world believe you're gone. It's one of the oldest fantasies there is, older than any of us, showing up in folklore long before it ever showed up in a police report. Tonight, we find out what actually happens if you try it. And whether L is the kind of detective who'd notice before you've even finished planning.

**STAKES FRAMING**

Here's the plan everyone assumes works: stage an accident, let the body never turn up, collect on the life insurance, start a new name somewhere warm. Simple. People do it in movies all the time, and it always looks easy on screen — one dramatic scene, a few months later a new identity, nobody the wiser.

Except almost nobody who's actually tried this in real life has gotten away with it. Not because the plan is bad — because the plan is exactly the same plan everyone else uses. There's a reason insurance investigators, forensic accountants, and missing persons detectives all recognize the shape of this case before they've even opened the file: it follows a template. And once you know the template, spotting the next person running it isn't hard at all.

This isn't a story about whether disappearing is possible. It's about how many separate places your plan has to work perfectly, all at once, for it to actually hold up — and how few people planning this ever stop to count how many places that actually is.

**THE SETUP**

Before L builds a case against you, this runs through four categories, not three — physical, digital, behavioral, and one people almost never think about until it's too late: who else knows.

Each category gets checked independently, by different people, using different methods, and they don't need to agree with each other completely. They just need to agree closely enough that someone notices the pattern forming across all four at once. Let's start with the one that looks, on the surface, hardest to fake convincingly — and turns out to be the easiest.

**PHYSICAL EVIDENCE**

You pick water. Almost everyone does — a lake, a river, a stretch of coastline with a real current. No body recovered means no autopsy, no dental records, no fingerprint match, no definitive proof of death at all. On paper, it's the cleanest option available to you, and it's the reason water shows up in the overwhelming majority of faked-death cases on record.

Your car gets found abandoned near the shoreline. Driver's door open. Personal effects still inside — a wallet, a phone, a jacket folded over the passenger seat. Just enough left behind to read as panic, not planning.

Except staged scenes have a tell that real accidents almost never do: they're too complete. A person who actually goes into open water in a genuine panic doesn't neatly fold their jacket first. They don't leave a wallet centered on the seat instead of jammed half-out of a pocket where it would naturally fall. They don't take their phone out and set it down — panic doesn't organize evidence, it drops it.

Every single item you deliberately leave behind to sell the story is one more piece of physical evidence that reads as arranged rather than chaotic. Investigators who work these cases aren't looking for what's missing. They're looking for what's too neatly placed — and a scene built to look convincing to a casual observer is often exactly what makes it fail under an expert's second look.

Then there's the timing. Most staged disappearances happen at a moment the person can control — a weekend, an empty stretch of road, low visibility, the kind of conditions that feel private. But an empty stretch of road with no witnesses at that exact hour is also, statistically, an unusual place for a genuine accident to occur completely unobserved. The absence of witnesses that made the scene convenient for you is the same absence that makes it read as suspicious once someone starts asking who else was around and why nobody was.

And weather matters more than people expect. A staged scene planned weeks in advance rarely accounts for the actual conditions on the day — water temperature, current strength, visibility — because the planning happened on a calendar, not against a forecast. Investigators cross-reference the environmental conditions logged that night against what the physical evidence claims happened, and a mismatch between the two is one of the quietest, most reliable red flags in the entire case file.

**THE DIGITAL PROBLEM**

Here's where it should fall apart completely — and it does, just not in the way you'd expect. Not because you were careless. Because the digital trail that seems the easiest to control is actually the hardest.

You bought a new life insurance policy eleven months ago. Not two years, not five — eleven months, right at the edge of most standard contestability periods, the window insurers specifically use to flag exactly this pattern. Insurance companies see this timing constantly, because almost everyone planning this underestimates how closely policy timing gets cross-referenced against cause of death. That single detail puts your file in front of a fraud investigator before your family has even received a check.

Your phone's last location ping sits close to the water, which looks right on its own — until someone checks how many other "accidental disappearances" also ping their last known location within a hundred meters of open water, at night, with the device never recovered. It's not unusual. It's the single most common last-known-location pattern in every faked-death case on file, which means it's the first thing anyone looking for a pattern already knows to check.

Then there's the silence itself. A phone that goes dark completely, all at once, with no partial data afterward — no accidental pocket-dial, no brief reconnection to a network, nothing — reads differently than a phone that's actually destroyed in an accident, which often still pings intermittently for hours as it degrades. A phone that goes from full signal to absolute nothing in one instant looks less like damage and more like deliberate shutdown.

Social media follows the same pattern in reverse. Genuinely tragic disappearances usually still generate some digital noise from people who don't know yet — a scheduled post that goes out anyway, a tagged photo from days earlier, a birthday reminder nobody's disabled. An account that goes completely, immediately silent right at the moment of disappearance, with every scheduled or automated activity also stopping precisely then, suggests someone actively managed the shutdown rather than simply vanishing.

And your financial accounts. Even if you stop using your main accounts entirely, a full stop is its own signal. Genuinely deceased people's accounts get closed through an estate process, with paperwork, with a death certificate, with a lawyer. Accounts that simply go dormant — no closures, no estate activity, just silence — sit in exactly the space fraud units are trained to flag, because real death generates administrative noise. Faked death generates none.

**BEHAVIORAL EVIDENCE**

The digital trail gets you flagged. The behavioral trail is what gets you actually caught.

In the weeks before you vanish, you do the things almost everyone in your position does without realizing they form a pattern. You quietly give away belongings you'd never normally part with — not everything, just enough that it feels responsible in the moment, and reads as exactly that in hindsight. You have unusually specific, unusually final conversations with people close to you — the kind that don't feel like goodbyes while you're having them, but sound unmistakably like goodbyes to anyone looking backward once they know what to listen for.

You search "how to start over with a new identity" from a device still logged into your real name, your real email, your real location history — because in the moment, using your own laptop feels completely ordinary. You don't yet think of yourself as a suspect, so you don't yet think to behave like someone being watched.

There's also the financial behavior beforehand — withdrawing cash in amounts just under the threshold that triggers automatic bank reporting, spaced out over several weeks rather than taken all at once. That spacing looks careful. It's also, itself, a documented pattern banks are specifically trained to flag, because genuine emergencies rarely require that exact kind of structured, incremental withdrawal.

None of these individual behaviors is illegal on its own. Giving things away isn't a crime. Neither is a heartfelt conversation, or a late-night search, or a cash withdrawal. But all of it together is the exact behavioral signature L has already seen a dozen times, because everyone planning to disappear behaves almost identically to everyone else who's ever planned to disappear. The fantasy is unique to you. The execution never is.

**THE ACCOMPLICE PROBLEM**

There's a fourth category most people planning this never account for: whether you did it alone.

Almost nobody actually can. Someone needs to know, even peripherally — a person who drives you somewhere, holds documents, forwards mail, or simply doesn't ask questions they should be asking. Every additional person who knows is a separate human being with their own phone, their own financial records, their own behavioral patterns that can be cross-referenced against yours.

That person's phone location the night you disappear. That person's sudden, unexplained change in spending right after. That person's own goodbye-shaped conversations with people close to them, because keeping a secret this large changes how a person acts even when they're not the one who disappeared. Investigators build a case around the suspect first, but they build the case that actually holds up in court around whoever else is connected to it — and a co-conspirator with weaker discipline than you is very often the actual point of failure.

The fewer people you tell, the harder your plan is to execute logistically. The more people you tell, the more surface area exists for the whole thing to unravel from someone else's mistake instead of your own.

**L NOTICES**

The insurance timing gets flagged internally before police are ever formally involved — a routine fraud referral, the kind that crosses a desk and usually goes nowhere. Except L doesn't treat it as routine. He cross-references it against the other categories before anyone's even called it a case: the suspicious last-location ping, the recent policy, the goodbye conversations flagged by a grieving family member who mentioned them almost in passing, and now — a financial irregularity connected to someone in your life who has no obvious reason to have one.

Four separate categories. Four separate people noticed something, independently, without comparing notes. That's the part that actually matters — L doesn't need a body to open an investigation. He needs a pattern that doesn't add up cleanly across all four, and this one doesn't.

**THE TRAP**

L doesn't wait for you to slip up. He creates a reason for you to slip up — and he does it twice.

**First attempt.** A small, deliberately leaked detail goes out through a local news outlet: an unidentified body was recovered near where your car was found, pending confirmation. If you're alive and watching from a distance — which almost everyone in this position eventually does, because staying away from your own case is far harder than people assume — that headline is built specifically to test exactly that instinct.

It works, halfway. Someone checks the article from an IP address nowhere near where you're supposed to be. But it's not enough to place you precisely — a VPN, a public network, no direct trace back to a physical address. What it does confirm is that you're alive, watching, and still emotionally invested enough in your own story to check on it.

**Second attempt.** With the location trace inconclusive, the next move targets the accomplice instead of you. A vague, unofficial rumor gets planted — nothing official, nothing that would hold up as evidence on its own — suggesting the investigation is quietly closing, no further action expected. If your accomplice believes the pressure is off, their behavior loosens. A phone call lasts slightly longer than it should. A payment gets sent through a method that's easier, less anonymous, because the perceived risk has dropped.

That's the entire point of a second trap: not to catch you directly, but to catch the discipline slipping in the person who was never as prepared for this as you were.

**THE SECOND ATTEMPT**

One failed trap doesn't close the file. Two narrows it considerably.

If your location can't be traced directly, the money can. A new identity costs money to maintain — new documents, new housing, day-to-day living — and money leaves a trail even when a person carefully doesn't. A single transaction in an unexpected location, using a payment method that traces back to an account you believed was fully separated from your old life, or worse, an account connected to the accomplice whose discipline just slipped.

That's the actual break: not a location, a signature. One purchase pattern that doesn't match anyone else who should reasonably have access to that account — a spending habit specific enough to you that it survives a name change even when nothing else does. It's not enough yet to know exactly where you are. It is enough to know you're still spending like yourself, under a different name, in a way that narrows the search from an entire country down to a specific region and a specific type of transaction.

From there, it's a matter of narrowing, not discovering. A region. A transaction type. A name that shows up near both. The investigation doesn't need a single dramatic breakthrough — it needs enough small, individually weak signals stacked on top of each other until only one explanation fits all of them at once.

**FINAL VERDICT + CTA + OUTRO**

Physical Evidence Trail: 4 out of 10. Digital Footprint: 7. Behavioral Pattern Consistency: 8. Environmental Control: 5. Adaptive Response: 6. Resource & Network Advantage: 3.

Nexus Detection Score: 33 out of 60.

You'd get flagged fast — faster than most people planning this ever expect. Whether you'd get found fast is a completely different question. The gap between "L knows you're alive" and "L knows exactly where you are" is the entire back half of this case, and it's a gap real people have lived inside for years, sometimes for the rest of their lives, without ever being fully located. The accomplice problem is usually what closes that gap — not your discipline slipping, but someone else's.

So here's the actual question, and it's the one worth sitting with: if you tried this yourself, would you have made it further than this? Or did you just watch your own plan fall apart in real time, one category at a time, the same way almost everyone else's does — including the one person you had to trust to help you?
