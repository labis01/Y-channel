# THE LOGIC NEXUS — Could L Catch Spider-Man?

Status: draft, ready to post
Format: 41-scene breakdown + a continuous narration version (TTS/voiceover-ready)

## Scene-by-scene script (original wording, 41 scenes)

SCENE 1
Peter Parker kneels beside a shattered fire hydrant, water pooling around his boots, wrapping a torn strip of synthetic webbing around a cut bleeding into his palm.

SCENE 2
He has no idea that three thousand miles away, a boy is sitting cross-legged on a hotel bed, staring at a laptop screen with a file open. His file. Right now, it's almost empty. That won't last.

SCENE 3
Here's the thing about L. He doesn't start with a face. He doesn't start with a name. He starts with a number. Before he's touched a single piece of evidence, he tells his handler there's a 74% chance the vigilante called Spider-Man can be identified within three weeks — and a 12% chance the whole case goes cold forever. He's not saying this to sound impressive. He's saying it because a number is a promise. And once you've made a promise out loud, you have to go find out exactly where reality disagrees with you.

SCENE 4
So let's set the rules, because this only works if both sides are playing fair. This is peak, mainline L — pre-captured, backed by the full weight of his agency, his handler running logistics behind the scenes, and access to surveillance systems most governments would kill for. But he's starting from zero. No prior knowledge of the masked vigilante. No special resource of his own. No home turf advantage in a city he's never worked before. Just a case file and a deadline he set for himself.

SCENE 5
Peter, meanwhile, is the MCU version — fresh off a recent life-altering event. A mysterious memory-erasing spell has already done its damage. Every person who ever loved him has forgotten he exists. He's alone, broke, exhausted, and still swinging through New York every single night, stopping crimes nobody even asked him to stop. Neither of them knows the other is out there. Not yet. That changes within the hour.

SCENE 6
L's greatest weapon has never been muscle or gadgets. It's patience — a flat, almost inhuman refusal to accept an answer before the evidence has earned it. Peter's biggest advantage is that an entire spell just erased the one thing every investigation depends on: a starting point. And that sets up the real question this video is going to answer — can pure deduction actually beat an identity that's been wiped clean?

SCENE 7
Picture L for a second. Not pacing, not barking orders into a phone. He's sitting with his knees pulled to his chest, unwrapping a candy he'll never actually eat, holding every possible answer in his head at once — refusing to let any one of them win too early. He never says "I believe." He says "there's a percentage," because belief is a decision, and a decision made too soon is the single most common way an investigation dies. Somewhere out there is a version of this case where L guesses wrong on day one and burns three weeks chasing a ghost. This isn't that version.

SCENE 8
Every investigation into Spider-Man starts in the same place: the residue. A jewelry heist in Midtown goes bad for the criminals when the masked vigilante tethers three of them to a delivery truck and disappears before police even arrive. Officers photograph the scene, but nobody thinks to preserve the tether-line — and within two hours, it dissolves anyway, the way it always does. That should be a dead end. It isn't.

SCENE 9
The detective doesn't need the physical tether-line. He needs footage of the exact moment it's fired, and Manhattan has never once run out of camera angles. He pulls forty-one hours of raw footage from the block — traffic cams, a bank's exterior feed, even a doorbell camera three buildings down that happens to catch a sliver of the fire escape. He isn't looking for a face. He's watching for one thing: the flick of a wrist right before a line fires. He times it. 0.14 seconds.

SCENE 10
Then he calculates the tension needed to support a grown man's swinging body weight from a fourth-floor anchor point without the line snapping — and the number tells him something nobody expected. Whoever built this understands material stress tolerances that most graduate engineers never even touch. This isn't some scavenged toy. It's a designed system, refined through failure after failure, which means somewhere in this city there's a workshop with a drawer full of earlier, worse prototypes. So the detective goes looking for the drawer.

SCENE 11
He cross-references patent filings, chemical supplier shipping records, and 3D-printing forums for a phrase almost nobody outside materials science ever types: "high-tensile short-cure polymer." Two things surface. A dead engineering blog, last updated fourteen months ago by a user calling themselves "prototype_dev4." And a bulk shipment of a specific industrial adhesive, sent to a residential address in a residential neighborhood, six months earlier. Neither is proof. Both are threads. And the detective, being who he is, doesn't pull on either one yet — not until he has more.

SCENE 12
Instead, he asks a different question. Who's paying for all this? Cartridges, filament, adhesive, fabric — none of it is free, and a person patrolling every single night can't exactly hold down a nine-to-five. He pulls anonymized gig-economy data from five different platforms across the neighborhood, filtering for accounts with strange, overnight-heavy activity spikes that line up with reported sightings. Fourteen accounts match. Fourteen, out of a city of millions. Still too many to act on — but no longer three hundred thousand strangers. Fourteen shapes in the dark. And he has learned, over and over, that shapes eventually become people, if you are patient enough to keep drawing them.

SCENE 13
Then, two days later, evidence lands in his lap by pure accident. During a warehouse robbery, the masked vigilante takes a glancing hit from a blunt object, and a small fragment of his tether-launcher housing shatters against the concrete. A patrol officer bags it as debris and files it away without a second thought. The detective requests every unclassified evidence bag logged inside his search radius that week — and finds it, buried in a box of unrelated scraps. The fragment isn't molded. It's machined, with tool marks that match a benchtop CNC router, not an industrial press. That narrows things fast.

SCENE 14
He pulls university lab-access logs and hobbyist maker-space memberships inside his radius, looking for anyone with unsupervised overnight access. It's not a name yet. But it's a shorter list of buildings. And a building is a lot easier to watch than a city.

SCENE 15
By day four, the detective stops chasing objects and starts studying behavior — because a punch, it turns out, reveals more about a person than a name ever could. He isolates every clean angle of unmasked combat he can find and slows it down frame by frame. The first thing that jumps out: restraint. In one clip, the masked vigilante catches a swinging car door with his bare hand — a force that should shatter a normal man's forearm — and instead of retaliating, he just pins the attacker's wrist gently to the pavement until police show up.

SCENE 16
The detective runs the numbers on the kinetic energy involved and reaches an uncomfortable conclusion: this person is deliberately fighting at a fraction of his real strength, every single time. That's not caution. That's a rule someone imposed on themselves. And people who build strict rules around their own violence are almost always carrying guilt about something they've already done.

SCENE 17
The second detail is stranger. In three separate clips, the masked vigilante dodges an incoming strike before it even lands. The detective rewinds one clip nine times. Same result every time — a flinch that begins before the physical stimulus exists. He doesn't write "precognition" in his notes. He writes "anticipatory threat detection, mechanism unknown," and moves on, because the how doesn't matter nearly as much as the what. If this person senses danger before it happens, a direct ambush is worthless. Any attempt to physically corner him fails before it even starts. That single realization quietly rewrites the entire investigation. The detective will never try to catch the masked vigilante. He's going to make the masked vigilante catch himself.

SCENE 18
The third clue costs the detective an entire afternoon rewatching interviews he almost skipped. Witnesses keep mentioning, almost sheepishly, that the masked vigilante cracks jokes mid-fight — like it's embarrassing for a superhero to act that way. He doesn't dismiss it. Compulsive humor under extreme stress is a well-documented coping mechanism, most common in people carrying repeated trauma with no support system around them. Add that to the restraint. Add it to the overnight gig-economy pattern. Add it to a formula that's amateur but obsessively refined. He writes one line in the file: subject is young, self-taught, isolated, guilt-driven, and treating vigilantism like either penance or the only structure left in an otherwise collapsed life.

SCENE 19
There's one more thread before the behavioral file closes. Older footage — a year and a half back — shows the masked vigilante in noticeably more advanced gear, arriving at scenes already coordinating with other costumed allies before police even arrive. The recent footage shows none of that. The suit's a single layer now, hand-stitched in places. And in every clip from the last several months, he arrives alone and leaves alone. Equipment doesn't downgrade by accident. And nobody voluntarily cuts ties with allies who could keep them safer — unless something severed those ties for them.

SCENE 20
The detective doesn't know about a spell. He doesn't need to. He only needs to know that whatever happened, it happened fast, it happened around eight months ago, and it left this person with nobody left to call before a fight. He still doesn't know Peter's name. But at this point, he probably knows Peter better than most of Peter's actual coworkers do. Sit with that for a second — he has never seen his face, never heard his real voice, never spoken to him once, and he can already predict, with real confidence, exactly how this kid will behave in a hostage situation, a stakeout, even a direct confrontation. That's not a lucky guess. That's the entire method working exactly the way it's supposed to — one small inference stacked on the next until a total stranger becomes, on paper, a fully modeled human being.

SCENE 21
By day six, the detective is done watching. It's time to test. Through an intermediary who has no idea what the real objective is, he arranges for a shipment of counterfeit passports to move through a warehouse right at the edge of his fourteen-account probability zone. The leak goes out through exactly one channel — a corrupt customs contact known to sell tips to vigilantes and journalists alike. No public announcement. He doesn't just want to know if the masked vigilante shows up. He wants to know how.

SCENE 22
Eleven minutes later, he gets his answer. The masked vigilante drops from a rooftop two blocks north — not the most direct route, a deliberate detour. Inside, he disables four armed men in under thirty seconds, not one wasted strike, then vanishes through a service exit instead of the way he came in. The hidden cameras catch nothing usable on his face, nothing conclusive on his height. But they catch something else — the exact rooftop he dropped from, and the direction he originally approached from before circling around. The detective draws two lines on a map. They cross three blocks inside his existing zone. The radius just shrank to six blocks.

SCENE 23
The masked vigilante doesn't know he's being tested. Not yet. But that's about to change. By day seven, small cracks start reaching him. A newsstand vendor mentions, almost in passing, that "a nice young man" was asking about the neighborhood's late-night businesses. A water tower where Peter sometimes rests between patrols has a fresh scuff mark near the ladder that wasn't there last week. A delivery cyclist he vaguely recognizes keeps parking on the same corner, two nights running, at hours that make no sense for deliveries. None of it proves surveillance. All of it is enough to make him uneasy — and Peter has survived multiple alien invasions by trusting exactly that kind of unease.

SCENE 24
So he adapts. He rides the subway several stops past his real destination and doubles back on foot. He stops using the same rooftop twice in a week. He gives up his favorite diner — the one with the only coffee he can actually afford — because a place you visit on a schedule is exactly the kind of pattern an investigator looks for. He's not wrong. That is exactly the pattern the detective is looking for. He just hasn't found it.

SCENE 25
Then Peter gets ambitious. Using spare tether-line fluid and a mannequin dragged out of a dumpster behind a closed department store, he rigs a crude pulley system between two rooftops in Hell's Kitchen — timed to launch the mannequin across the gap every fifteen minutes, mimicking a tether-line swing. For two nights, it actually works. The detective's team flags a "sighting" nearly forty blocks outside the real search area, and resources shift to chase it.

SCENE 26
The detective watches the footage twice before something nags at him. The figure's silhouette shifts posture mid-arc in the exact same way, three separate times — no variation for wind, no variation for momentum. A real body under real physics never repeats itself that precisely. Within the hour, he's labeled it a mechanical decoy and pulled his people back. But he doesn't count those two nights as wasted. Because a target capable of building a working decoy on short notice is a target who now knows, for certain, that someone is watching him closely enough to be worth misdirecting. The investigation hasn't stalled. It's escalated — on both sides, at the exact same time.

SCENE 27
That single insight becomes the center of the detective's next eight days. He stops chasing patrol routes and starts asking a much colder question: what does this person refuse to give up, even now? He goes back to his fourteen accounts and cross-references them against recurring, non-work visits to the same civilian locations. Four names get eliminated immediately; their build, height, or age don't match the physical profile. Three more fall away once he quietly confirms alibis through workplace attendance records. Six more show no consistent civilian pattern at all. That leaves one. A single account with repeated, almost ritualistic visits, several nights a week, always brief, always the same late hour — to a small twenty-four-hour bodega, two blocks from a building with a suspiciously thin paper trail behind its lease.

SCENE 28
The detective sends an observer posing as a delivery courier to watch the bodega for three nights. On the second, a young man walks in, orders the same sandwich he's clearly ordered a hundred times before, trades small talk with the owner, and leaves. The owner doesn't know his name. But the observer catches something small — a barely-there stiffness in the kid's left side, consistent with a still-healing rib injury after a hard impact, not a fall. The detective checks the timeline. Three nights earlier, the masked vigilante took a direct hit from a car during a highway pileup rescue. The window lines up within eleven hours. It isn't proof. It's a resemblance, and he has learned the hard way that resemblances are worthless until you force them to either collapse completely or hold up under pressure.

SCENE 29
Before he commits to that address, he almost commits to the wrong one. A second name inside the same six-block radius — a delivery cyclist with a similarly thin employment history — briefly jumps ahead in his internal percentages after a neighbor mentions seeing him "moving strangely fast" one night. He pours eighteen hours of surveillance into that lead before a single detail collapses it: the cyclist was photographed, timestamped, sitting in a hospital emergency room the same night the masked vigilante was seen three miles away stopping a robbery in progress. He doesn't call those eighteen hours wasted. He calls it proof the method is working exactly as intended — killing off confident-sounding wrong answers before they can quietly calcify into assumptions nobody thinks to question again.

SCENE 30
So he widens the net around the real apartment, carefully, without ever approaching directly. Public records list the tenant as Peter Parker. Lease started less than a year ago. No prior rental history. No high school transcripts before an abruptly completed GED. No steady job — just scattered freelance gig work. It's not an empty file. It's a suspiciously recent one — the kind that looks less like a redacted record, and more like a life rebuilt from nothing, in the exact window of time the entire city forgot the masked vigilante's face.

SCENE 31
The detective doesn't move yet. Suspicion built entirely from absence isn't something he trusts. He needs Peter to hand him something he can't later explain away. So he spends two more days cross-referencing encrypted NYPD dispatch timestamps against every recorded arrival. And it fits, cleanly, with everything else. The masked vigilante consistently beats dispatched units that are more than three blocks out — but almost never beats units already within one block. Which means he isn't intercepting the calls. He's just already out there, close enough to hear the sirens start on his own. It rules out an informant or a stolen scanner, and it locks the profile onto someone physically present in the neighborhood, not someone watching from a screen somewhere else. Every wrong theory he eliminates narrows the case just as much as every right one confirms it.

SCENE 32
Meanwhile, in an apartment neither of them has connected to the other yet, Peter sits on the edge of his mattress at four in the morning, still half in his suit, listening to a city that no longer remembers he exists for it. He has no idea a name is closing in on him. He only knows something has felt wrong for two weeks straight — and that the wrongness hasn't once made him consider stopping. There's nobody left to tell him he's pushing too hard. So he tells himself, quietly, and goes back out anyway. Because sitting still with everything the spell took from him is worse than the risk of getting caught.

SCENE 33
The break the detective's been waiting for arrives on day thirteen — and he doesn't engineer it. Peter does, simply by being who he is. A residential fire breaks out four blocks from his narrowed radius, half past two in the morning. Black smoke pours from a fourth-floor window. A stairwell collapses one level below. Two parents are already out on the fire escape, calling for a grandmother still trapped inside. The masked vigilante arrives in ninety seconds — the fastest response the detective has ever clocked from him — and goes straight through the window instead of waiting for a ladder.

SCENE 34
Seconds later he's back out, a coughing elderly woman held safely against his chest, handing her down before anyone can even get a statement. It's the fastest, least cautious he's moved in almost two weeks of careful evasion. And the detective understands exactly why. Whatever discipline Peter's built to protect his own identity evaporates instantly the second someone else's life is the price of keeping it.

SCENE 35
That one decision gives the detective his cleanest geographic fix of the entire case. The masked vigilante could only have entered from that direction if he started less than ninety seconds away, on foot or by direct leap, from inside the exact six-block radius already suspected. Add the bodega. Add the fresh lease. Add a healing rib injury on a young man who otherwise avoids drawing any attention at all. The case has stopped being a probability. Now it's a decision — how does he confirm this without triggering a confrontation he can't physically win?

SCENE 36
He spends the better part of a day just weighing the approach. Every version of a physical ambush ends badly in his calculations, given the precognitive reflex he clocked back in week one. Every version that waits for more evidence risks giving Peter time to notice the folder that's already been quietly assembled — and vanish completely. The only version that actually works is the one where Peter never feels cornered enough to run in the first place. So he picks somewhere public. Somewhere quiet. The same bodega Peter visits out of pure habit, on a slow weekday afternoon when the shop is nearly empty. No backup. He takes the small counter by the window, orders tea he has no intention of drinking, and waits.

SCENE 37
Eleven minutes later, Peter walks in — exhausted in that specific, bone-deep way that has nothing to do with sleep. He orders his usual sandwich, trades his usual small talk with an owner who has no idea how many times this exact moment has already been quietly recorded from across the street, and turns to leave. That's when the detective says his name. Out loud. For the first time. Peter's shoulders lock before he's even fully turned around. It isn't fear that freezes him in place. It's the specific, sinking recognition of a guess landing exactly where it shouldn't have been able to reach.

SCENE 38
He doesn't run. Running would only confirm what a stranger couldn't yet prove on his own — and some exhausted, calculating part of him has already figured that out. He turns slowly, sets his bag down, and asks the only question that actually matters. "What do you want?" The detective doesn't answer right away. He's too busy updating a percentage that, for the first time in thirteen days, has finally hit one hundred.

SCENE 39
Instead of a response, he slides a folder across the counter — a move he's rehearsed a dozen times in his head, never quite believing he'd need it. Inside: a still from the warehouse test, a photo of the mechanical decoy mid-collapse, and a single address, circled in red. No threats. He doesn't need any. He just lets Peter see, in full, exactly how completely his every precaution has already been mapped, measured, and accounted for. Peter doesn't read past the first page. He doesn't have to. He already knows what's in it. Whatever happens next, at least it isn't a stranger guessing. It's someone who actually did the work.

SCENE 40
So — could the detective catch the masked vigilante? Yes. Not through one flash of brilliance, but through thirteen relentless days of narrowing an entire city down to a single sandwich order that an exhausted kid couldn't bring himself to give up. Working purely off percentages, patience, and Peter's own inability to stop being a hero even when it cost him the one thing he had left — the detective would have a name in roughly nine to fourteen days.

SCENE 41
And the most unsettling part isn't how fast that is. It's this: the exact qualities that make Peter Parker worth protecting — his compassion, his restraint, his refusal to let anyone else pay for his safety — are the same qualities that made him impossible to hide. Every countermeasure Peter built was a defense against being found by force. None of them were ever built to survive being found by patience. And patience was the only weapon the detective ever actually needed.

He gives up his favorite diner—the one with the only coffee he can actually afford—because a place you visit on a schedule is exactly the kind of pattern an investigator looks for. He's not wrong. That is exactly the pattern the detective is looking for. He just hasn't found it.

Then Peter gets ambitious. Using spare tether line fluid and a mannequin dragged out of a dumpster behind a closed department store, he rigs a crude pulley system between two rooftops in Hell's Kitchen—timed to launch the mannequin across the gap every fifteen minutes, mimicking a tether line swing. For two nights, it actually works. The detective's team flags a "sighting" nearly forty blocks outside the real search area, and resources shift to chase it. The detective watches the footage twice before something nags at him. The figure's silhouette shifts posture mid-arc in the exact same way, three separate times—no variation for wind, no variation for momentum. A real body under real physics never repeats itself that precisely. Within the hour, he's labeled it a mechanical decoy and pulled his people back. But he doesn't count those two nights as wasted. Because a target capable of building a working decoy on short notice is a target who now knows, for certain, that someone is watching him closely enough to be worth misdirecting. The investigation hasn't stalled. It's escalated—on both sides, at the exact same time.

That single insight becomes the center of the detective's next eight days. He stops chasing patrol routes and starts asking a much colder question: what does this person refuse to give up, even now? He goes back to his fourteen accounts and cross-references them against recurring, non-work visits to the same civilian locations. Four names get eliminated immediately; their build, height, or age don't match the physical profile. Three more fall away once he quietly confirms alibis through workplace attendance records. Six more show no consistent civilian pattern at all.

That leaves one. A single account with repeated, almost ritualistic visits, several nights a week, always brief, always the same late hour—to a small twenty-four-hour bodega, two blocks from a building with a suspiciously thin paper trail behind its lease. The detective sends an observer posing as a delivery courier to watch the bodega for three nights. On the second, a young man walks in, orders the same sandwich he's clearly ordered a hundred times before, trades small talk with the owner, and leaves. The owner doesn't know his name. But the observer catches something small—a barely-there stiffness in the kid's left side, consistent with a still-healing rib injury after a hard impact, not a fall.

The detective checks the timeline. Three nights earlier, the masked vigilante took a direct hit from a car during a highway pileup rescue. The window lines up within eleven hours. It isn't proof. It's a resemblance, and he has learned the hard way that resemblances are worthless until you force them to either collapse completely or hold up under pressure.

Before he commits to that address, he almost commits to the wrong one. A second name inside the same six-block radius—a delivery cyclist with a similarly thin employment history—briefly jumps ahead in his internal percentages after a neighbor mentions seeing him "moving strangely fast" one night. He pours eighteen hours of surveillance into that lead before a single detail collapses it: the cyclist was photographed, timestamped, sitting in a hospital emergency room the same night the masked vigilante was seen three miles away stopping a robbery in progress. He doesn't call those eighteen hours wasted. He calls it proof the method is working exactly as intended—killing off confident-sounding wrong answers before they can quietly calcify into assumptions nobody thinks to question again.

So he widens the net around the real apartment, carefully, without ever approaching directly. Public records list the tenant as Peter Parker. Lease started less than a year ago. No prior rental history. No high school transcripts before an abruptly completed GED. No steady job—just scattered freelance gig work. It's not an empty file. It's a suspiciously recent one—the kind that looks less like a redacted record, and more like a life rebuilt from nothing, in the exact window of time the entire city forgot the masked vigilante's face.

The detective doesn't move yet. Suspicion built entirely from absence isn't something he trusts. He needs Peter to hand him something he can't later explain away. So he spends two more days cross-referencing encrypted NYPD dispatch timestamps against every recorded arrival. And it fits, cleanly, with everything else. The masked vigilante consistently beats dispatched units that are more than three blocks out—but almost never beats units already within one block. Which means he isn't intercepting the calls. He's just already out there, close enough to hear the sirens start on his own. It rules out an informant or a stolen scanner, and it locks the profile onto someone physically present in the neighborhood, not someone watching from a screen somewhere else. Every wrong theory he eliminates narrows the case just as much as every right one confirms it.

Meanwhile, in an apartment neither of them has connected to the other yet, Peter sits on the edge of his mattress at four in the morning, still half in his suit, listening to a city that no longer remembers he exists for it. He has no idea a name is closing in on him. He only knows something has felt wrong for two weeks straight—and that the wrongness hasn't once made him consider stopping. There's nobody left to tell him he's pushing too hard. So he tells himself, quietly, and goes back out anyway. Because sitting still with everything the spell took from him is worse than the risk of getting caught.

The break the detective's been waiting for arrives on day thirteen—and he doesn't engineer it. Peter does, simply by being who he is. A residential fire breaks out four blocks from his narrowed radius, half past two in the morning. Black smoke pours from a fourth-floor window. A stairwell collapses one level below. Two parents are already out on the fire escape, calling for a grandmother still trapped inside.

The masked vigilante arrives in ninety seconds—the fastest response the detective has ever clocked from him—and goes straight through the window instead of waiting for a ladder. Seconds later he's back out, a coughing elderly woman held safely against his chest, handing her down before anyone can even get a statement. It's the fastest, least cautious he's moved in almost two weeks of careful evasion. And the detective understands exactly why. Whatever discipline Peter's built to protect his own identity evaporates instantly the second someone else's life is the price of keeping it.

That one decision gives the detective his cleanest geographic fix of the entire case. The masked vigilante could only have entered from that direction if he started less than ninety seconds away, on foot or by direct leap, from inside the exact six-block radius already suspected. Add the bodega. Add the fresh lease. Add a healing rib injury on a young man who otherwise avoids drawing any attention at all. The case has stopped being a probability. Now it's a decision—how does he confirm this without triggering a confrontation he can't physically win?

He spends the better part of a day just weighing the approach. Every version of a physical ambush ends badly in his calculations, given the precognitive reflex he clocked back in week one. Every version that waits for more evidence risks giving Peter time to notice the folder that's already been quietly assembled—and vanish completely. The only version that actually works is the one where Peter never feels cornered enough to run in the first place.

So he picks somewhere public. Somewhere quiet. The same bodega Peter visits out of pure habit, on a slow weekday afternoon when the shop is nearly empty. No backup. He takes the small counter by the window, orders tea he has no intention of drinking, and waits.

Eleven minutes later, Peter walks in—exhausted in that specific, bone-deep way that has nothing to do with sleep. He orders his usual sandwich, trades his usual small talk with an owner who has no idea how many times this exact moment has already been quietly recorded from across the street, and turns to leave.

That's when the detective says his name. Out loud. For the first time.

Peter's shoulders lock before he's even fully turned around. It isn't fear that freezes him in place. It's the specific, sinking recognition of a guess landing exactly where it shouldn't have been able to reach. He doesn't run. Running would only confirm what a stranger couldn't yet prove on his own—and some exhausted, calculating part of him has already figured that out. He turns slowly, sets his bag down, and asks the only question that actually matters.

"What do you want?"

The detective doesn't answer right away. He's too busy updating a percentage that, for the first time in thirteen days, has finally hit one hundred. Instead of a response, he slides a folder across the counter—a move he's rehearsed a dozen times in his head, never quite believing he'd need it. Inside: a still from the warehouse test, a photo of the mechanical decoy mid-collapse, and a single address, circled in red. No threats. He doesn't need any. He just lets Peter see, in full, exactly how completely his every precaution has already been mapped, measured, and accounted for.

Peter doesn't read past the first page. He doesn't have to. He already knows what's in it. Whatever happens next, at least it isn't a stranger guessing. It's someone who actually did the work.

So—could the detective catch the masked vigilante? Yes. Not through one flash of brilliance, but through thirteen relentless days of narrowing an entire city down to a single sandwich order that an exhausted kid couldn't bring himself to give up. Working purely off percentages, patience, and Peter's own inability to stop being a hero even when it cost him the one thing he had left—the detective would have a name in roughly nine to fourteen days.

And the most unsettling part isn't how fast that is. It's this: the exact qualities that make Peter Parker worth protecting—his compassion, his restraint, his refusal to let anyone else pay for his safety—are the same qualities that made him impossible to hide. Every countermeasure Peter built was a defense against being found by force. None of them were ever built to survive being found by patience. And patience was the only weapon the detective ever actually needed.
