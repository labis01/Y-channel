**[0:00–0:15] COLD OPEN**

Two detectives. Neither has ever lost a case they were willing to finish. Tonight, we put them in the same file — and find out which one actually deserves the title.

**[0:15–1:07] Stakes Framing**

Here's what you'd expect: the one with global surveillance access and unlimited processing power wins this instantly. Track the tech, trace the signal, case closed in an afternoon. That's the obvious answer, and it's the one most people would put money on before they've seen a single piece of evidence.

It's also wrong. The digital trail on this subject isn't just hard to find — it's completely empty, in a way that almost never happens by accident. And an empty trail from someone this capable isn't a dead end. It's the loudest piece of evidence in the entire file, once you know how to read it.

**[1:07–1:30] The Setup**

Before either detective gets credit for anything, this case runs through three categories the same way any real investigation would — physical, behavioral, digital. One of them will flip everything you think you know about who's actually ahead. Let's start with the one that looks the most ordinary.

**[1:30–4:01] Physical Evidence**

No fingerprints — the gloves see to that. But gloves can't erase everything, and that's where this starts: with what's left behind by accident, the details nobody plans around.

A boot impression, recovered at four unrelated crime scenes across the city, all carrying the same reinforced tread pattern — not a commercial sole, something custom-built. Grapple-line entry marks on rooftops, always the same tension, always the same angle of approach, consistent enough to reconstruct the exact launch mechanism behind it down to the millimeter.

A torn fiber snagged on a windowsill during a struggle — a Kevlar weave that doesn't match anything sold to civilians or law enforcement, meaning someone is manufacturing it privately, off the books, in quantities too small to show up on any industrial supply record.

Then there's the timing of the injuries themselves — not just their pattern, but when they occur relative to the incident. Every documented case shows the same three-to-five second gap between first contact and full incapacitation, a window too consistent to be adrenaline or luck. That's the signature of thousands of repetitions, not a single gifted amateur having a good night.

Even the injuries on the people he catches follow a pattern — controlled, repeatable trauma. Dislocations instead of breaks. Compression instead of impact force that would be lethal at that speed. Someone spent years learning exactly how much damage stops a person without risking a manslaughter charge, and that's not a skill anyone picks up by accident.

This is what a physical case file is supposed to look like — five separate details, all pointing the same direction: this was never improvised. But physical evidence only tells you what someone did. It doesn't tell you who has the resources to disappear afterward — and that's where this case stops being ordinary.

**[4:01–6:04] The Digital Problem**

Here's where you'd expect the whole thing to break open. It doesn't.

No digital footprint. No purchase records for the equipment, under any traceable identity, in any jurisdiction investigators checked. No signal to trace back to a source — because someone erased it before the investigation even started looking, clean enough that even forensic recovery tools find nothing but gaps where data should exist. Standard financial tracing software isn't built to handle a subject with zero transaction history at all — most fraud-detection systems flag unusual activity, not the complete absence of any activity, which means this case doesn't even register as suspicious to the tools built to catch people like this.

That absence is the actual finding. You don't build technology at this level without money moving somewhere, without a facility, without a supply chain that has to touch the physical world eventually — even when nothing digital survives to prove it. A footprint this clean isn't incompetence. It's proof of exactly how much this person had to lose if anyone ever found it, and how long they've had to plan for exactly this kind of investigation.

So the investigation stops looking for a signal and starts looking for what the signal was built to hide — and that single shift is what turns an unsolvable digital trail into the sharpest lead in the whole case. Which raises a very different question: what kind of person plans that far ahead, for a threat that might never even materialize?

**[6:04–8:05] Behavioral Evidence**

Location data answers it. It draws a map, and this map isn't random.

Four districts get protected far more than the rest of the city, consistently, across every year of recorded incidents — not just more often, but with a response time that beats official emergency services by an average of six minutes. Targets aren't chosen at street level either — the pattern skips the dealers entirely and goes straight for the lieutenants running them, which means whoever's doing this reads an organizational chart, not headlines, and updates that chart faster than most intelligence units manage to.

Incidents cluster around specific windows, always avoiding maximum witness exposure — operational discipline most vigilantes never bother with, since most people in this line of work want to be seen. This one doesn't. And notably: nothing ever happens near a handful of specific buildings downtown. Not because there's nothing there. Because avoiding them matters more than the pattern would otherwise predict — which suggests a personal connection to those locations that has nothing to do with crime statistics at all.

That takes access most people patrolling a city alone shouldn't have. Which means the profile built from three categories of evidence isn't describing a person yet — it's describing a method. And the moment that method gets close enough to feel like a person, everything about this case changes.

**[8:05–9:48] Batman Notices**

For the first time, the subject stops being passive.

A route used twice becomes a route never used again. A pattern that was starting to hold simply stops repeating overnight. Equipment that should show wear from documented incidents shows none — duplicates, built specifically to defeat this exact kind of tracking, meaning whoever's behind this anticipated forensic analysis before it ever started.

Then it escalates. A trap gets set based on the established behavioral pattern — a staged incident, in one of the four protected districts, timed to draw a response using the exact profile built from months of data. It works — someone shows up, right on schedule, exactly where the pattern predicted. But there's no confrontation, no capture opportunity. Just confirmation the response was clocked, followed immediately by every future pattern shifting again, as if the entire profile had been quietly discarded the moment it proved useful to someone else.

He didn't just notice he was being read. He started reading the investigation back. This isn't a one-sided case anymore — it's two methodical people, both trained to notice everything, working against each other's next move in real time, each adjustment a message to the other side that the game has changed.

**[9:48–11:38] The Trap**

With behavioral prediction compromised, the next trap has to be built around something that can't change overnight — the supply chain behind the technology itself. A single sourced material, ordered once, delivered to exactly one facility, tied to a component that takes months to redevelop if swapped out, a rare enough compound that global demand for it can be tracked almost supplier by supplier.

The plan is simple on paper: don't wait for Batman. Wait for whoever's authorizing the purchase. It almost works — surveillance confirms unusual activity matching the acquisition timeline down to the day, a vehicle tracked leaving under conditions matching every prior pattern, right down to the route and the hour. For a moment, this looks closed, the kind of clean resolution that almost never happens this early in a case like this.

Then the vehicle turns out to be a decoy, sent specifically to test whether the facility was being watched — a deliberate, expensive gesture just to confirm one piece of information. By the time that's confirmed, the real facility is already dark, equipment gone, nothing left but proof the theory was right all along. Right method. Wrong moment — the kind of near-miss that only happens when both sides are operating at exactly the same level, and neither one is willing to move first without being certain.

**[11:38–13:16] The Second Attempt**

One failed trap doesn't end a case like this. It refines it. If a facility can move, a material can't disappear from every supplier at once without someone noticing the silence — supply chains, unlike people, leave a trail even when nobody's trying to hide it deliberately.

That's the actual break. Not a location — a gap. One supplier who stops selling a specific compound entirely, right around the week the original facility went dark, with no public explanation and no obvious business reason behind it. Nobody walks away from a profitable product voluntarily unless it was worth their while to vanish from the paper trail completely, and that kind of silence only happens when someone pays considerably more than the product is actually worth.

Following that silence instead of following movement finally produces something real: a secondary facility, smaller, less protected, clearly a fallback rather than the primary operation — built in a hurry, without the same layers of misdirection as everything before it. Not the endgame. But the first piece of this case that wasn't anticipated and countered in advance, and the first sign that even this level of preparation has a limit.
