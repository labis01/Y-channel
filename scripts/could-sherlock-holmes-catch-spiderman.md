# THE LOGIC NEXUS — Could Sherlock Holmes Catch Spider-Man?

Status: draft, ready for review
Format: scene breakdown + continuous narration version (TTS/voiceover-ready)
Runtime target: ~20 minutes

Second entry in the "Could ___ Catch Spider-Man?" series (see
`could-l-catch-spiderman.md` for the first). Same MCU Spider-Man state
carries over: post-memory-wipe, alone, no allies, exhausted, still
patrolling every night. The challenger this time is peak Sherlock Holmes —
deliberately built to contrast with L's data-driven digital surveillance:
Holmes works through physical trace evidence, personal observation, and a
human informant network rather than servers and cameras, so the episode
doesn't feel like a reskin.

---

## Scene-by-scene script

SCENE 1
A city bus, brakes gone, fishtails toward a crowded crosswalk. A web-line catches the rear axle a half-second before impact, hauls it sideways into an empty loading zone. By the time bystanders look up, there's nothing on the rooftops but a torn strip of red fabric caught on a fire escape bolt, still swinging.

SCENE 2
Three thousand miles away, in a borrowed room above a Manhattan precinct, a man in a rumpled coat is handed a file so thin it barely needs a folder. He doesn't open it right away. He turns it over once, weighs it in his hand like the emptiness itself is information, and only then starts reading.

SCENE 3
"Three weeks," he says, before he's finished the first page. "Possibly two, if the city cooperates." His handler, a liaison who's read every psychological profile the department has on him, doesn't ask how he can be so certain from four paragraphs of description. He's learned not to. A guess is a hope wearing a disguise. What Holmes just gave her wasn't a guess. It was an estimate, and an estimate is allowed to be wrong in a way a guess never earns the right to be.

SCENE 4
So let's set the terms, because a fair fight needs fair terms. This is peak Holmes, transplanted whole into the present day — his methods, his mind, his contempt for assumption, dropped into a city he's never worked, with none of the local knowledge that made Baker Street feel like an extension of his own skull. No case files on the vigilante beyond a folder of secondhand witness accounts. No forensic lab beyond what a consulting badge can borrow for a few hours at a time. Just his eyes, his chemistry set, and a self-imposed deadline he intends to beat out of pure irritation at the idea of missing it.

SCENE 5
Peter, unchanged from where we left him — the MCU version, still living in the wreckage of a memory-erasing spell that convinced an entire world it never knew him. No allies to call. No one to notice if he doesn't come home some night. Just a mask, a fading skill with a sewing needle, and a compulsion to keep saving people who couldn't identify him in a lineup of one.

SCENE 6
Here's the difference that actually matters. L hunted through data — patterns in servers, gaps in gig-economy platforms, footage rewound frame by frame. Holmes doesn't trust a screen he can't smell. He hunts through residue, fabric, gait, and the particular way a lie sounds different from a guess in a witness's mouth. Same question as before, asked from the opposite direction: can a method built for gaslight and hansom cabs actually out-think a target modern surveillance was never built to catch in the first place?

SCENE 7
Picture him for a second, because he's not what people expect. Not pacing, not muttering theories aloud for effect. He stands very still at the edge of a crime scene, hands behind his back, and says almost nothing for the first ten minutes. Everyone else is asking what happened. He's the only one asking what's missing. He's fond of saying that people see a room a hundred times and never once observe it. He intends to observe this city until it hands him a name.

SCENE 8
His first stop isn't a database. It's the loading zone where that bus stopped fishtailing, kneeling over a patch of pavement where the web-line struck. He doesn't photograph it. He touches it, rolls a trace of the residue between thumb and forefinger, holds it to the light, and finally to his nose. Whatever the fluid is, it's not a factory formula — it dries tacky at the edges and carries a faint bitter-almond note that no commercial adhesive he's encountered ever has.

SCENE 9
That single detail tells him more than a lab report would. Someone cooking a compound like this in small batches, refining it by feel rather than by specification sheet, is self-taught and doing it somewhere with poor ventilation — a home setup, not a facility. He doesn't need a patent search. He needs a chemist's nose, and a city full of hardware stores that sell the same handful of base compounds to anyone with cash.

SCENE 10
Two streets over, the fabric snagged on the fire escape gives him a second thread — literally. Under a jeweler's loupe borrowed from a pawnbroker who owes him nothing but agrees anyway, the weave is uneven, the stitching hand-done in places where a machine would have been faster and neater. Whoever made this suit values function over appearance, and doesn't have access to — or interest in — proper tailoring equipment. Amateur, but not careless. A person building something that has to work, not something that has to look finished.

SCENE 11
Holmes doesn't chase this fabric through a database either. He walks it, literally, into three fabric wholesalers within a mile of the sighting, showing the scrap to clerks who've worked their counters for a decade and would recognize their own stock blind. Two shrug. The third — an elderly man who sells synthetic-weave remnants to costume shops and cosplayers — pauses just half a second too long before saying he doesn't recognize it. Holmes doesn't push. He simply notes the address and moves on, because a nervous non-answer is worth more, followed later, than a false one chased down immediately.

SCENE 12
What he builds next isn't a surveillance net. It's a favor economy. Newsstand vendors. Late-shift doormen. Cab drivers who've spent forty years learning which fares are worth remembering. Delivery cyclists who see more of a neighborhood at 3 a.m. than any patrol car ever will. He pays some in cash, some in solved problems of their own — a missing nephew located in an afternoon, a false accusation quietly disproven — and asks all of them the same simple question: tell me anything strange, no matter how small.

SCENE 13
The first tip comes within two days. A rooftop water-tower repairman mentions, almost as an afterthought, a set of shoe impressions in the dust up top — smaller stride, deeper impact on landing than a grown man his apparent size should leave. Holmes climbs up himself to measure them by hand: stride length, depth, spacing. He walks away with an estimate of build and age that no photograph could have given him nearly as precisely, because photographs flatten what footprints preserve.

SCENE 14
He interviews witnesses in person, not through transcripts. He wants their hesitations, their unconscious gestures, the moment their story slows down because they're second-guessing what they actually saw. One man, describing a mugging interrupted, keeps circling back to the same detail without realizing it: the masked man could have broken his attacker's arm and chose, very deliberately, not to.

SCENE 15
Holmes writes one line in his own hand, no percentage attached: restraint this consistent isn't temperament, it's discipline — and disciplined restraint around violence is what a person builds when they're already afraid of what they're capable of. Not caution. Penance rehearsed so many times it's become reflex.

SCENE 16
A second witness, describing an entirely different incident, mentions the masked man ducking a swung pipe "before I even finished swinging it — like he knew." Holmes doesn't write precognition. He writes a colder, more useful phrase: reacts to threat before the threat completes its motion, mechanism unclear, treat as reliable. If the theory holds, an ambush is worthless before it starts. He crosses out an entire early plan to intercept the vigilante directly, and doesn't look back at it once.

SCENE 17
That single correction reshapes the whole case. He was never going to trap this man with force. He's going to make the man's own habits do the trapping instead — the oldest trick in his own methodology, dressed up in modern clothes.

SCENE 18
A pattern from the informant network catches his attention next: three separate witnesses, unprompted, mention the masked man making jokes mid-fight, almost embarrassed about it afterward. Holmes has seen this before, in soldiers back from campaigns nobody wanted to discuss. Humor under real danger isn't confidence. It's a pressure valve for a person carrying more weight alone than any one person should. Add it to the restraint. Add it to the amateur, self-funded equipment. The profile sharpens: young, self-taught, isolated, and treating this like the only structure left in a life that's otherwise come apart.

SCENE 19
An older cab driver, one of the few who's worked this neighborhood for over a decade, mentions offhand that "the fella used to have friends helping him out, a year or two back — capes, gadgets, the lot. Hasn't seen any of them in ages." Holmes asks nothing further. Equipment doesn't get worse by choice, and nobody voluntarily loses a support network that was keeping them safer. Something took it from him, recently enough that the wear is still showing on his gear.

SCENE 20
Holmes doesn't know about a spell. He'll never need to. He only needs to know that whatever happened, it happened fast, cost this young man everyone he could once call for help, and hasn't stopped him going back out every single night regardless. On paper, he now knows this stranger's psychology better than most people know their own neighbors — without once having seen his face.

SCENE 21
By the end of the first week, observation alone won't move the case any further. Holmes needs the target to act, on his terms. Through one of the cab drivers — a man known for being unable to keep a secret past a second fare — he lets slip a rumor about a jewel shipment moving through a specific warehouse district that same night. He tells no one else. He wants to see not just whether the masked man shows, but exactly how he arrives.

SCENE 22
He watches from a rented room across the street with nothing more advanced than a pair of opera glasses and a stopwatch. Eleven minutes after the rumor should have reached its target, a shape drops from a rooftop two blocks past the obvious approach — a deliberate, unnecessary detour. Four armed men are down in under a minute. Holmes doesn't need footage. He has a compass bearing, a timed interval, and a radius that's just meaningfully shrunk.

SCENE 23
The masked man doesn't know he's being tested, not yet — but something about the last few days has put him on edge. A newsstand vendor who's never once made conversation suddenly wants to talk about the neighborhood. A doorman he doesn't recognize watches a beat too long before looking away. None of it proves anything. All of it is enough. He's survived worse than a hunch, and he's learned to trust the hunch anyway.

SCENE 24
So he changes his habits — new rooftops, staggered timing, longer routes home. He starts timing his patrols to overlap with fire truck responses, so any pattern reads as coincidence rather than schedule. It's a smart adjustment. It's also, unknowingly, exactly the kind of behavioral shift a man like Holmes is trained to notice precisely because it's new.

SCENE 25
Then Peter does something Holmes doesn't expect. Instead of just evading, he starts hunting his hunters — quietly following two of the more obvious "vendors" home, confirming they're being paid to watch specific corners. One of Holmes's own informants, a fifteen-year-old delivery cyclist, gets a very polite, very unsettling visit on his own rooftop that ends with a single sentence: "Tell whoever's asking that I know they're asking."

SCENE 26
Holmes takes the message exactly as it's meant. He pulls the informant network back immediately rather than risk it collapsing entirely, and accepts the cost as data rather than defeat: a target sharp enough to identify a passive surveillance net without ever seeing a face behind it is a target whose intelligence just moved from theoretical to confirmed. The investigation doesn't stall. It simply changes its only remaining tool back to the one Holmes trusts most — his own legwork.

SCENE 27
He spends the next stretch of days doing what he'd have done a century earlier without a second thought: walking the six-block radius the warehouse test narrowed him to, canvassing shopkeepers, memorizing faces, asking after anyone with strange hours and no obvious job. He rules out four candidates in two days on build alone. Three more on confirmed daytime employment that leaves no room for the vigilante's documented hours.

SCENE 28
One name briefly rises to the top after a landlord mentions a tenant who "moves like an athlete" — until Holmes confirms, through a hospital intake ledger a sympathetic clerk lets him glance at, that the same man was admitted with a broken collarbone on a night the masked man was seen, uninjured, three miles away. Holmes doesn't call the day wasted. A wrong answer eliminated cleanly is worth exactly as much as a right one narrowed.

SCENE 29
The break comes from a bodega owner two blocks inside the radius, who mentions a regular customer — quiet, always the same order, always late — who's been favoring his left side for the better part of a week, like a bruised rib he's trying not to show. Holmes checks the timeline against the last reported sighting of the vigilante taking a hard hit during a highway rescue. The window matches within hours. Not proof yet. A resemblance worth testing until it either breaks or holds.

SCENE 30
A quiet afternoon at the local land registry office — a favor owed by a solicitor Holmes once helped clear of a false charge — turns up a lease taken out under the name Peter Parker less than a year earlier. No prior tenancy on record. No employment history beyond scattered freelance gig work. It reads less like a redacted file and more like a life built again from nothing, in exactly the window everyone in this city apparently forgot the masked man's face.

SCENE 31
He doesn't move on the name yet. Absence isn't evidence, only invitation. So he cross-references response timing one final time, this time by hand against precinct dispatch logs a friendly desk sergeant lets him review — and finds the same pattern L would later independently confirm through digital means: the masked man consistently beats responding units that start more than a few blocks out, and almost never beats the ones already close. He isn't intercepting calls. He's already out there, within earshot of the sirens before they finish starting.

SCENE 32
Meanwhile, in an apartment that has no idea it's already been found on paper, Peter sits on the edge of his bed near four in the morning, half out of his suit, listening to a city that doesn't know it owes him anything. He doesn't know a name is closing in. He only knows the unease hasn't lifted in days, and that stopping has never once felt like the safer option.

SCENE 33
The final piece arrives, as it so often does, because Peter can't help being exactly who he is. Scaffolding collapses on a downtown construction site just past midnight, trapping two workers beneath a support beam no ordinary rescue team can lift in time. The masked man arrives in under two minutes — faster, more careless of being seen, than in nearly two weeks of careful evasion — and lifts the beam alone, in full view of a dozen phones already filming.

SCENE 34
Holmes doesn't need the footage to know what it means. Whatever discipline this young man has built to protect his own privacy collapses the instant someone else's life depends on it. That single decision gives Holmes the tightest fix of the entire investigation — a two-minute response radius that overlaps almost perfectly with a bodega, a fresh lease, and a healing rib injury, all belonging to the same unassuming young man.

SCENE 35
He spends a full day simply deciding how to approach, because every version involving force ends badly against a reflex that reacts before a threat even completes. Every version that waits risks losing the advantage of surprise entirely. The only approach that survives his own scrutiny is the one where the young man never feels cornered enough to run in the first place.

SCENE 36
So he chooses the bodega — quiet, familiar, public — on a slow afternoon when it's nearly empty. He takes a seat near the window, orders tea out of habit rather than intention, and waits without checking his pocket watch once.

SCENE 37
Peter walks in eleven minutes later, tired in the specific way that has nothing to do with sleep. He orders the usual, trades the usual small talk, turns to leave — and stops, because a stranger he's never met says his name like it isn't a question.

SCENE 38
He doesn't run. Some exhausted, calculating part of him understands immediately that running now would only confirm what a stranger couldn't yet prove alone. He sets his bag down and asks the only thing that matters: "What do you want?" Holmes doesn't answer right away. He's occupied revising a three-week estimate down to an actual number — eleven days, two ahead of his own original guess.

SCENE 39
Instead of an answer, he slides a small, worn notebook across the table — pages of measurements, sketches of footprints, a fabric sample pinned beside a timeline. No threats inside it. None needed. He simply lets Peter see, page by page, exactly how thoroughly an ordinary city was made to give up a stranger's whole life without a single camera ever catching his face.

SCENE 40
So — could Sherlock Holmes catch Spider-Man? Yes. Not through a single flash of brilliance, but through eleven relentless days of trusting residue, fabric, footprints, and human testimony over anything a screen could offer him. Working from nothing but observation, patience, and a young man's inability to stop being a hero even when it cost him the one advantage he had left — Holmes would have a name in a week and a half, ahead of his own deadline, exactly as he intended.

SCENE 41
And the detail that should unsettle you isn't how fast that is. It's this: Holmes built his entire career on a single accusation aimed at everyone he ever met — that people see, but they do not observe. Peter Parker spent two weeks assuming that wearing a mask was the same thing as being unseen. It never was. It only meant that everything he actually did — the restraint, the jokes, the exhaustion, the refusal to stop — was left in plain sight for a man who'd made a career of noticing exactly what everyone else walks past.

He simply lets Peter see, page by page, exactly how thoroughly an ordinary city was made to give up a stranger's whole life without a single camera ever catching his face.

So — could Sherlock Holmes catch Spider-Man? Yes. Not through a single flash of brilliance, but through eleven relentless days of trusting residue, fabric, footprints, and human testimony over anything a screen could offer him. Working from nothing but observation, patience, and a young man's inability to stop being a hero even when it cost him the one advantage he had left, Holmes would have a name in a week and a half, ahead of his own deadline, exactly as he intended.

And the detail that should unsettle you isn't how fast that is. It's this: Holmes built his entire career on a single accusation aimed at everyone he ever met — that people see, but they do not observe. Peter Parker spent two weeks assuming that wearing a mask was the same thing as being unseen. It never was. It only meant that everything he actually did — the restraint, the jokes, the exhaustion, the refusal to stop — was left in plain sight for a man who'd made a career of noticing exactly what everyone else walks past.
